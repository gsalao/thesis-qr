\section{Results and Evaluation}
\label{header:results}

\paragraph{}This section presents the empirical evaluation of the implemented prototype along the five-phase validation pipeline established in Section~\ref{sect:meth4}: cryptographic correctness (Phase~1), penetration testing and performance benchmarking (Phase~2), an initial User Acceptance Test (UAT Cycle~1, Phase~3), a targeted refinement pass driven by Cycle-1 feedback (Phase~4), and a second User Acceptance Test on the refined prototype (UAT Cycle~2, Phase~5). The cryptographic and benchmark phases were conducted on the localhost build of the prototype, whose architecture is identical to the production deployment described in Section~\ref{sect:meth4_d}; the only material difference between the two environments is the addition of a TLS termination layer at the cloud edge, which does not affect the cryptographic protocol itself but does add fixed network round-trip overhead. All artifacts referenced in this section (i.e., raw test logs, statistical summaries, and the unaltered output of the benchmark scripts) are reproduced under the project's \texttt{logs/} directory and are referenced inline by listing.

\subsection{Phase 1 --- Functional and Cryptographic Validation}
\label{sect:results_func}

\paragraph{}Cryptographic correctness was assessed through a Vitest-based unit suite (under \texttt{server/tests/} as \texttt{functional.test.js}) that isolates the BLS12-381 primitives (i.e., hash-to-curve, partial signing, Lagrange-based aggregation, and bilinear-pairing verification) from the network and persistence layers. The suite executes thirty-five (35) tests across seven (7) categories and completed in 707~ms on the development machine. Results are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:functional}.

\paragraph{}Particularly relevant to the threshold-correctness claim is Category~4: every one of the $\binom{5}{3} = 10$ possible 3-of-5 signer combinations was tested for end-to-end aggregation. Each subset successfully reconstructed the joint secret through Lagrange interpolation and produced an aggregate signature $F_{\text{sig}}$ that satisfied the bilinear pairing equation
\[
e(F_{\text{sig}},\, g) \;=\; e(H(M),\, PK).
\]

\paragraph{}Conversely, Category~5 confirmed the dual property: every 2-of-5 combination tested failed verification, since two evaluation points on the degree-2 polynomial $f(x) = \text{secret} + x + x^2$ uniquely determine a degree-1 line that does not pass through $f(0) = \text{secret}$. The Lagrange interpolation produces an arithmetically valid but cryptographically incorrect scalar, which the pairing equation rejects. This is the precise mathematical property that grounds the threshold-security claim of the protocol; a coalition of fewer than $t$ shareholders cannot forge a signature even with full knowledge of their own shares.

\paragraph{}Tamper-resistance (Category~6) addressed the integrity guarantee. Six payload attacks were attempted: amount tampering ($\textit{amount}: 2500 \to 1$), nonce replacement, timestamp zeroing, two forged-byte signatures (random hex and all-zero), and cross-transaction signature reuse. All six were rejected by the verifier, either by the curve membership check inside the underlying \texttt{@noble/curves} library (for malformed points such as the random and all-zero forgeries) or by the pairing equation itself (for valid points produced from a different message digest). In every case, the rejection was fail-closed: the verifier returned \texttt{false} without exposing any partial information about which check had failed, removing the oracle that an attacker would otherwise need to refine forged inputs.

\subsection{Phase 2a --- Performance Benchmarks}
\label{sect:results_perf}

\paragraph{}The latency benchmark, executed via \texttt{server/tests/latency-test.js}, measures the two performance-critical operations: (i) \textbf{QR generation}, defined as the time from the \texttt{request\_transaction} emission to receipt of \texttt{transaction\_initiated} with status \texttt{COMPLETED}; and (ii) \textbf{payment verification}, the time from the \texttt{verify\_payment} emission to receipt of \texttt{payment\_processed}. Each trial used a single low-value transaction (₱100--₱109, threshold $t = 1$) with a 300~ms inter-trial delay to avoid lock contention on the per-transaction mutex. Thirty (30) consecutive trials were collected.

\paragraph{}\textbf{a.) Verification latency.} The principal acceptance criterion of Section~\ref{sect:meth4_c} --- has a sample mean of $4.83$~ms and a 99th-percentile worst case of $8$~ms across 30 trials. Every trial completed in well under $200$~ms; the 99th-percentile margin against the target is $200 / 8 = 25\times$. This margin is large enough that even on the production cloud deployment, where geographic routing on the free-tier hosting introduces an additional $50$--$150$~ms of round-trip overhead, the total verification time is expected to remain well within the criterion.

\paragraph{}\textbf{b.) QR generation.} This is the more expensive of the two operations because it bundles three cryptographic primitives, namely, BLS partial signing, Lagrange-based aggregation, and bilinear-pairing self-verification, with a sequence of MongoDB writes (\texttt{Transaction.create}, \texttt{Transaction.save}, \texttt{GroupBalance.findOneAndUpdate}, \texttt{AuditLog.save}). Even so, the mean of $24.73$~ms and the p99 of $32$~ms remain comfortably below the $100$~ms threshold conventionally taken as the upper bound of imperceptible response in interactive systems. \paragraph{}A complementary observation concerns the BLS aggregation step itself. Because BLS signatures aggregate into a single compact group element regardless of the number of signers, the \textit{post}-aggregation payload size is invariant in $t$; only the pre-aggregation message complexity grows linearly with $t$, and that growth is absorbed entirely on the server. This is a structural advantage over multi-signature schemes that concatenate independent signatures into the QR payload (e.g., stacked RSA), where increasing $t$ inflates QR data density and consequently scan latency, as further discussed in Section~\ref{sect:disc_qr}.

\subsection{Phase 2b --- Adversarial / MITM Validation}
\label{sect:results_mitm}

\paragraph{}Resilience against the threat model of Section~\ref{sect:meth4_c} was evaluated through the script under \texttt{server/tests/} as \texttt{mitm-test.js}, which exercises thirteen (13) distinct attack scenarios partitioned into an offline cryptographic group (A1--A7) and an online protocol group (B1--B6). All thirteen scenarios resolved as expected; in each adversarial case the attack was blocked, and in the two positive controls (A7 and B4) the legitimate request was correctly accepted. Results are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:mitm}.

\paragraph{}\textbf{Offline cryptographic attacks (A1--A7)} target the pairing-verification logic directly without going through the WebSocket transport. A1 and A2 exercise the elliptic-curve membership check inside \texttt{@noble/curves}: a candidate pair $(x, y)$ that does not satisfy the BLS12-381 curve equation is rejected before the pairing computation begins, surfaced internally as \texttt{Error: bad point coordinate x} (random bytes) or \texttt{Error: bad point coordinate y} (all-zero bytes). A3--A5 hold the signature constant while perturbing the message digest $H(M)$ via amount tampering, nonce replacement, or cross-transaction signature reuse; in each case the pairing equation breaks because the signature was bound to a different digest. A6 directly tests the threshold property by aggregating only two of the five shares and presenting the result as if it were a complete signature; the Lagrange interpolation produces a scalar that does not equal the joint secret, and the pairing fails. A7 confirms the positive control: a legitimate aggregate of three shares verifies.

\paragraph{}\textbf{Online protocol attacks (B1--B6)} target the verifier through the same Socket.io interface a real client would use. B1 submits a freshly generated UUID as a transaction nonce; the verifier responds \texttt{``Transaction not found or has expired from records.''} since no matching ledger entry exists, demonstrating that nonce uniqueness alone is sufficient to defeat the simplest replay variants. B2 and B3 are the most economically meaningful attacks in the suite: an adversary who has compromised the WebSocket transport may attempt to inflate or deflate the deducted amount by submitting a forged \texttt{amount} field together with an otherwise valid (replayed) nonce. The current implementation deducts only the amount stored server-side at the time of transaction creation; the client-supplied value is logged but ignored. Test~B2 confirmed that a ₱999{,}999 inflation attempt against a ₱100 transaction resulted in exactly ₱100 being deducted; B3 confirmed the symmetric deflation case (₱1 submitted against a ₱500 transaction $\to$ ₱500 deducted). B5 verifies that a previously paid transaction (status \texttt{PAID}) cannot be re-verified; B6 verifies that a transaction whose threshold has not yet been met (status \texttt{PENDING}) cannot be paid out prematurely.

\paragraph{}\textit{A note on disclosure.} The amount-tampering vulnerability that motivated tests B2 and B3 was discovered during this study and patched in \texttt{server/controllers/socketHandler.js} prior to the performance benchmarking reported in Section~\ref{sect:results_perf}. The pre-fix handler used \texttt{data.amount} (the client-supplied value) as the operand of the balance deduction, exposing the system to an attacker who had broken the transport layer (e.g., via a rogue certificate authority or a downgrade attack on a non-TLS instance). The patched handler reads the authoritative amount from the persisted \texttt{Transaction} document, removing the client controllability of that field. The B2 and B3 tests were authored specifically to provide regression coverage for this fix.

\paragraph{}On the production deployment (\texttt{https://thesis-qr.onrender.com}), all Socket.io traffic uses WebSocket Secure (\texttt{wss://}) over TLS 1.3. A network-level adversary intercepting TCP packets observes only ciphertext, so the offline cryptographic attacks A1--A7 and the protocol attacks B1--B6 effectively assume an attacker who has already broken TLS, which is a strictly stronger threat model than a passive eavesdropper. The fact that all thirteen scenarios are still defeated under that stronger assumption is, in the view of the authors, the most consequential security claim of the present study.

\subsection{Phase 3 --- UAT Cycle 1 (Initial Usability)}
\label{sect:results_uat1}

\paragraph{}Cycle~1 followed the protocol set out in Section~\ref{sect:meth4_uat1}. A first cohort of five (5) participants exercised the prototype as it existed at the conclusion of Phase~2, in which the QR code carried its entire authorisation payload inline. In this initial encoding (subsequently termed the ``Big QR'' variant), the joint public key $PK$, the curve generator $g$, the aggregate signature $F_{\text{sig}}$, and the transaction message $M$ are serialised into the QR matrix directly, producing a self-contained, in-band cryptographic proof that the verifier can validate without contacting any external service. Cycle~1 was therefore the first occasion on which the protocol's intended encoding was placed in front of real users.

\paragraph{}Each participant was assigned a phone role (Shopper, Approver, or Cashier) and walked through the ten task scenarios listed in the UAT instrument. Every scenario produced the expected outcome (\textbf{10 / 10 PASS}), including the race-condition case (Scenario~4: three approvers tap simultaneously on a ₱2{,}000 request; the system accepts the first two valid approvals and ignores the third), the double-scan-prevention case (Scenario~5), and the timeout / QR-expiry case (Scenario~9, in which the Shopper's screen showed ``request expired'' after the two-minute QR TTL had elapsed).

\paragraph{}The principal Cycle-1 signal arose not from a scenario failure but from the open-ended feedback (Section~7.1 of the instrument), which converged with notable consistency on the dynamic QR code's two-minute expiry window. Four of the five participants asked, in their own words, for a clearer indication of remaining QR validity: respondents requested a ``timer for [the] expiring QR code'', an ``optimise[d] QR expiry'', ``more explicit timers (graphic or numerical)'', and a timer ``visible instead of just verifying that time is up''. The fifth participant instead asked for a notification mechanism so that ``new transactions'' would be noticeable ``without [a] face-to-face meeting''. In their overall impressions (Section~7.2), all five participants were broadly positive: the system was described as ``very fast in terms of approvals'' and ``simple and seamless'', with one participant noting they would adopt it if it ``existed within GCash or other banking apps''.

\paragraph{}Read together, the Cycle-1 responses indicated that the dominant friction in the interaction flow was not cryptographic or computational --- the benchmarks of Section~\ref{sect:results_perf} had already established sub-$30$~ms backend latency --- but \textit{temporal-perceptual}: participants were uneasy about whether a generated QR would still be valid by the time it was scanned, and the interface gave them no continuous, legible cue of the remaining window. This finding is what the Phase-4 refinement was scoped to address.

\subsection{Phase 4 --- UI Refinement and Bug Fixes}
\label{sect:results_refine}

\paragraph{}Following the decision rule of Section~\ref{sect:meth4_refine} --- that refinements must be confined to the interaction and encoding layers so as not to invalidate the Phase~1 and Phase~2 evidence --- the Cycle-1 feedback was triaged into two principal refinements, both targeting the QR-expiry time pressure.

\paragraph{a.) Visible countdown timer.} The most direct response to the Cycle-1 feedback was to render an explicit, continuously updating countdown of the QR code's remaining validity on the Shopper's screen, replacing the previous design in which expiry was only confirmed after the fact. This change is purely presentational: it surfaces a value (\texttt{qrExpiresAt}) that the protocol already maintained, and alters neither the two-minute time-to-live nor any cryptographic field.

\paragraph{b.) Reduced QR payload (the ``Small QR'' variant).} A second, complementary refinement addressed the same time pressure from the opposite direction: rather than only making the remaining window legible, it shortened the time the window is consumed by scanning. The refinement introduced an alternative encoding --- subsequently the ``Small QR'' variant --- in which the authorisation envelope is identified by a short reference token, with the cryptographic content retrieved out of band by the verifier on scan over the same authenticated channel the protocol already trusts. Because the token is far shorter than the full $\{PK, g, F_{\text{sig}}, M\}$ envelope, the Small QR is encoded at a substantially lower QR version with a sparser module grid, which reduces the camera-autofocus burden at scan time. Crucially, this is an \textit{encoding-layer} change only: the BLS12-381 primitives, the threshold policy, the message format, the AES-256-GCM session encryption, and the pairing verification predicate are all unchanged, and the Phase~1 unit suite and Phase~2 MITM script continued to pass against the refined build. Figure~\ref{fig:qr-comparison} contrasts the two encodings at equal physical scale.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{images/fig_qr_comparison.png}
  \caption{The two QR encodings compared across the two UAT cycles, rendered at equal physical scale. The Big QR (used in Cycle~1 and by Group~A of Cycle~2) carries the full $\{PK, g, F_{\text{sig}}, M\}$ authorisation envelope in-band, producing a denser module grid. The Small QR (introduced in Phase~4 and used by Group~B of Cycle~2) carries only a short reference token, with the verifier retrieving the same envelope out of band at scan time.}

\paragraph{}Cycle~2 was then designed to evaluate the refined build and, in particular, to compare the two QR encodings directly: the second cohort was split into a Group~A that retained the Big QR and a Group~B that used the new Small QR, with both groups receiving the visible countdown timer.

\subsection{Phase 5 --- UAT Cycle 2 (Final Usability)}
\label{sect:results_uat2}

\paragraph{}Cycle~2 followed the same instrument and the same ten scenarios as Cycle~1, applied to the refined build. A second cohort of ten (10) participants was recruited, disjoint from the five Cycle-1 participants, and split into two groups of five: \textbf{Group~A} ($n=5$) exercised the Big QR encoding and \textbf{Group~B} ($n=5$) exercised the Small QR encoding introduced in Phase~4. Across all fifteen UAT participants (five in Cycle~1, ten in Cycle~2), no participant took part in more than one session. As in Cycle~1, every scenario produced the expected outcome in both groups (\textbf{10 / 10 PASS} for each group), including the race-condition, double-scan, and expiry cases.

\subsubsection{System Usability Scale (SUS)}
\label{sect:results_uat_sus}

\paragraph{}A six-item adaptation of the System Usability Scale~\cite{brooke1996sus} was administered to all ten Cycle-2 participants. Five items were positively keyed (frequency of intended use, ease of use, integration of functions, learnability, and felt confidence/security) and one item was negatively keyed (need for technical support to operate the system). Following the standard SUS convention~\cite{brooke1996sus, bangor2008sus}, each positive item contributes ($\textit{rating} - 1$) and the negative item contributes ($5 - \textit{rating}$); the six per-item contributions, each in $[0, 4]$, are summed and normalised:
\[
\textit{SUS}_{6} \;=\; \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{6} c_i}{6 \times 4} \times 100.
\]
Per-participant scores are reported in Table~\ref{tab:sus}.

\paragraph{}The aggregate six-item SUS across the ten Cycle-2 participants is $77.9$, which sits in the upper half of the conventional SUS range and just above the $68$-point average commonly taken as the threshold of acceptable usability~\cite{bangor2008sus}. The two groups, however, diverge sharply: Group~A returned a mean of $95.0$, well into the ``excellent'' band, while Group~B returned $60.8$, below the conventional average.

\paragraph{}This divergence is \textit{not} interpreted as an effect of the QR encoding, for two reasons. First, the two groups are disjoint and small ($n = 5$ each), so any between-group difference is confounded with participant composition. Second --- and decisively --- the QR-recognition item (Section~\ref{sect:results_uat_qr}) shows the Small QR group scanned at least as fast as the Big QR group, so a scannability penalty cannot account for the lower Small QR SUS. The Group~B mean is instead pulled down by two participants who paired low ratings with specific, substantive critiques rather than with any usability defect: one returned the lowest score in the study while raising a pointed concern about sub-threshold transaction splitting (discussed in Section~\ref{sect:results_uat_open}), and another returned a guarded, middling rating across all items. The SUS result is therefore reported as the single $n = 10$ aggregate of $77.9$, with the per-group split shown in Table~\ref{tab:sus} for transparency but explicitly not attributed to the encoding variable. The Cycle-1 cohort, which exercised the Big QR, returned SUS responses consistent with the Cycle-2 Big QR group's high band.

\subsubsection{Latency and Scannability Perception}

\paragraph{}Three survey items captured the user-perceived temporal experience. On item~4.1 (approval speed), all ten Cycle-2 participants selected ``Instant / Seamless'', confirming that the sub-$30$~ms backend latency measured in Section~\ref{sect:results_perf} is experienced as immediate. \paragraph{}Every Small QR participant reported immediate recognition; among the Big QR participants, four reported immediate recognition and one reported a 2--3 second focus delay. The direction of this difference is consistent with the well-documented relationship between QR module density and camera-autofocus latency --- a denser matrix requires the autofocus controller more frames to settle~\cite{corpuz2025} --- and the Big QR carries an order of magnitude more data than the reference-token Small QR. It should therefore be read as a modest confirmation of an expected effect rather than as a strong empirical contrast, and the Cycle-1 Big QR cohort, whose recognition responses were consistent with the Cycle-2 Big QR group, does not materially change that reading.

\subsubsection{Perceived Security and Open-Ended Feedback}
\label{sect:results_uat_open}

\paragraph{}On item~5.1 (``Multi-Party Approval''), all ten Cycle-2 participants selected either ``Yes, significantly safer'' (7 of 10) or ``Somewhat safer'' (3 of 10) when asked whether the requirement for multi-party approval increased their perception of safety against unauthorised spending; no participant selected ``No difference'' or ``No, it felt less secure'' (Figure~\ref{fig:uat-safety}). On item~4.3 (perceived friction), nine of ten participants chose ``A little extra effort, but worth it for the added security'', with a single participant selecting ``Too much effort for daily use''. This finding is consistent with the literature on mental models of multi-party authorisation~\cite{komlo2021}, which reports that the visible enforcement of an approval quorum tends to dominate perceived security over the underlying cryptographic mechanism.

\paragraph{}The open-ended feedback (Section~7.1 of the instrument) from the Cycle-2 cohort converged on four recurring themes, none of which concerned the QR-expiry timer that had dominated Cycle~1:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A deposit / fund-replenishment feature.} Four of the five Big QR participants independently asked for a deposit flow, observing that the prototype currently models only the spending side of a joint account. The requests ranged from a bare ``deposit feature'' to a ``deposit feature for sustainability'' and a pairing of depositing with member-attribution (``add names/accounts to keep track of who [is] involved''). This is the single most frequent suggestion in the study and is treated as Recommendation~\ref{sect:rec_deposit}.
  \item \textbf{Stricter credential confirmation at the point of approval.} Three participants observed that, in the current prototype, possession of an unlocked phone is functionally equivalent to consent to sign. Their suggestions spanned all three authentication categories: a ``face recognition'' check ``to make sure [it is] really the person who approved'', ``login credentials (username + password)'', and an ``added authentication layer \dots\ in the form of biometric authentication, one-time pins, and etc.'' This is treated as Recommendation~\ref{sect:rec_mfa}.
  \item \textbf{Out-of-band approval notifications.} Two participants noted that, because the prototype requires all members to be concurrently logged in, a pending approval ``[is] not easy to notice'' and in practice would need ``a third-party app like Messenger'' to alert co-signers. One framed this as a UX issue (pending approvals being easy to miss when scrolled off-screen under a time limit); the other framed it as a structural limitation. This is treated as Recommendation~\ref{sect:rec_notify}.
  \item \textbf{Sub-threshold transaction splitting.} One participant raised a pointed security critique: because transactions below ₱1{,}000 require no group approval ($t = 1$), a malicious member could ``circumvent the security offered by the system by simply committing several transactions worth [₱]999''. The participant explicitly conditioned their recommendation of the system on this being handled. This is a genuine design gap; it is discussed in Section~\ref{sect:disc_limits} and addressed by Recommendation~\ref{sect:rec_velocity}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{}On overall impressions (Section~7.2), the Cycle-2 responses were predominantly but not uniformly positive. Most participants stated they would use or recommend the system, citing the multi-party consensus mechanism (``each member is provided with equal responsibility'') and its simplicity and ease of use; one volunteered that it ``can be very effective when integrated with actual online accounts''. Two responses were qualified rather than positive: one participant declined to judge the system, citing no prior experience with joint accounts, and the participant who raised the sub-threshold-splitting concern offered only a conditional recommendation, contingent on that flaw being addressed. The honest summary is therefore that the system was well received, with two substantive reservations that map directly onto the recommendations of Section~\ref{header:recs}.

\subsection{Consolidated Evaluation}
\label{sect:results_summary}

\paragraph{}Table~\ref{tab:results-summary} consolidates the headline outcomes against the acceptance criteria stated in Section~\ref{sect:meth4}. Every quantitative correctness, performance, and adversarial criterion was satisfied with substantial margin. The user-acceptance criteria were satisfied with one nuance: scenario coverage was complete in both UAT cycles, perceived-security responses were uniformly positive, and the aggregate SUS exceeded the conventional acceptability threshold, but the SUS aggregate ($77.9$) is materially lower than the figure an inspection of the Big QR cohort alone would suggest, owing to the critical Group~B responses discussed in Section~\ref{sect:results_uat_sus}.
